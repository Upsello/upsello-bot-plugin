# Ad brief

## Six things a brief must answer

1. **Who it is for**: one audience, in one sentence (age + situation + what they are weighing right now)
2. **Which point it makes**: one real reason people did not buy, or one real desire, taken from the team's records — not guessed
3. **What the first three seconds say**: the first line or the first frame decides whether the creative lives
4. **What the proof is**: specs, comparison, demonstration, reviews — only what the store already has
5. **What you want them to do**: open the product page, take the coupon, add to cart — pick one
6. **What it may not say**: this creative's red lines

## Where angles come from

Highest priority first:

1. Real objections the team recorded (price, shipping, size, trust, timing)
2. Negative reviews and return reasons, when they exist
3. Claims competitors repeat in their ads (to position against, never to copy)
4. Common buying motives in the category (a gift, replacing an old one, an emergency)

**An angle without evidence does not go into a brief.** Better one fewer creative than an invented "pain point".

## Writing the first three seconds

- State the result: "This jacket is fine at minus 20"
- State the conflict: "Stop buying sheets that fade"
- Show the comparison: old against new, before against after
- Ask the question: "Has this happened to you too?"

Do not open with: a brand introduction, a logo animation, or "Hi everyone, I'm…".


## Red lines

- No promised outcomes (cures, whitening, permanent)
- No invented reviews, certificates or test reports
- No superlatives unless there is something checkable behind them
- No discounts or countdowns that do not exist
- No borrowed footage or someone else's likeness

## Before it runs

1. State what is being tested: the angle, or the audience
2. Start small, and change one variable at a time
3. Changing a budget, pausing an ad or going live needs the user's explicit approval
4. Record the result: which angle, which audience, what it cost
